Abstract

Self-supervised speech models are a strong baseline for speech emotion recognition, but they still confuse emotions whose prosody is similar, such as happy and sad. We combine five speech pipelines built on wav2vec 2.0 and emotion2vec with a facial-expression model built on MediaPipe blendshapes, using temperature-calibrated late fusion, and evaluate everything with 6-fold actor-wise cross-validation on RAVDESS, so every one of the 24 actors is tested once on models that never saw them. Fusion improves every speech model, by 5.3 to 7.7 accuracy points (all 95% confidence intervals above zero). The best system, LoRA-adapted wav2vec 2.0 with the face model, reaches 78.5%, against 73.2% for speech and 60.4% for the face alone. Under a matched probe and training recipe, frozen wav2vec 2.0 and frozen emotion2vec are not reliably different. On the published actor folds of Luna-Jiménez et al., the face model alone is within a few points of published face-only results.

Method

Two independent models, one per modality, each produce a score (logit) for every emotion. The speech branch is a self-supervised encoder with a small classifier head; the face branch summarises the facial blendshapes from MediaPipe Face Landmarker over the clip and classifies them with an XGBoost random forest. Each branch’s logits are divided by a temperature fitted on the validation actors and turned into probabilities, so neither branch dominates by scale alone, and the two sets of probabilities are summed:

y^= arg⁡maxc [ softmax(z(a)Ta)c + softmax(z(v)Tv)c ]

Speech models

  • wav2vec 2.0, weighted-layer probe. wav2vec 2.0 [1] learns speech representations by self-supervised masked prediction on unlabelled audio; we use the wav2vec2-base checkpoint. The probe follows Pepino et al. [3], who found that emotion cues are spread across the encoder’s layers: a learned softmax weighting combines all 13 hidden states (the CNN feature encoder and the 12 transformer layers), each frame passes through a 128-unit dense layer, and the frames are mean-pooled and classified.
  • wav2vec 2.0, LoRA. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning as in Lashkarashvili et al. [4]: LoRA adapters of rank 4 on the attention’s query and value projections, their weighted sum of all layers and a learned sigmoid gate on the hidden features, then LayerNorm and one linear layer. The LoRA settings follow the paper and were not tuned.
  • emotion2vec, final-layer probe. emotion2vec [2] is pretrained by self-supervised distillation on emotional speech. We freeze emotion2vec_base and train a 128-unit classifier head on its final layer. We avoid the emotion2vec+ checkpoints, which are fine-tuned with a teacher that has seen RAVDESS.
  • Matched probes, wav2vec 2.0 and emotion2vec. To compare the two encoders fairly, both are frozen and given the same probe (a learned weighting of all 13 hidden states, then a 128-unit head; 99,348 trainable parameters each), the same training recipe and the same 4-setting hyperparameter grid, selected on validation actors. Each clip runs through the encoder at its true length, so no padding enters the average.

The weighted-layer probe and LoRA wav2vec 2.0 models use mild SpecAugment-style masking (about 5% of time steps and feature channels) so that short prosodic cues survive. All models are trained with class-weighted cross-entropy, AdamW with a cosine schedule, and early stopping on validation loss.

Data and face features

  • Data. The RAVDESS speech subset (1440 clips, 24 actors), evaluated with 6-fold actor-wise cross-validation. Each fold tests 4 actors (2 male, 2 female), validates on 4 others and trains on the remaining 16, so every actor is tested once, on models that never saw their voice or face. Model selection and fusion temperatures use the validation actors only.
  • Seeds. The systems that train on cached frozen features, the face model and their fusions are repeated with 3 seeds; the two wav2vec 2.0 systems that train with the encoder in the loop use one seed.
  • Labels. Seven emotions: angry, disgust, fearful, happy, neutral, sad and surprised, with calm merged into neutral.
  • Face features. Blendshapes keep facial-action intensities and drop background, lighting and appearance. Each clip is summarised by 7 statistics (mean, standard deviation, max, min, range, mean and max velocity) for each of 53 channels, the 52 ARKit blendshapes plus MediaPipe’s _neutral score, and 5 ratios between face regions, then classified by an XGBoost random forest. Neural sequence models on the raw blendshapes overfit in early tests.

Findings

  1. Does adding the face help? Yes, for every speech model: +5.3 to +7.7 accuracy points over all 24 actors, with every confidence interval above zero, and in every test fold for three of the five models. The face model is strong where speech is weak (happy) and weak where speech is strong (surprised, fearful). On actors whose faces it reads poorly (fold 3), equal-weight fusion can lower accuracy.
  2. Does emotion-specific pretraining win? No, and it does not lose either. With the same probe, recipe and tuning budget, frozen wav2vec 2.0 is 3.3 points ahead of frozen emotion2vec, but the difference is within the uncertainty (95% CI −0.6 to +7.0) and each wins three of six folds.
  3. Frozen or LoRA wav2vec 2.0? The LoRA pipeline beats the frozen-probe pipeline, by 6.2 points alone and 4.1 points with the face model. The two pipelines also differ in their classifier heads, and LoRA was run with one seed.

How good is the face model?

Run alone on the exact five actor folds and eight classes (calm kept apart from neutral) of Luna-Jiménez et al. [7], the blendshape face model reaches 57.9%, against 62.13% for their OpenFace action units with a bi-LSTM and 60.22% for their static MLP. The face features differ, and no held-out actors were used for model selection here. Calm and neutral are rarely confused from the face (4–8% of clips); neutral is mistaken for sad more often. The report has the details.

Limitations. The two wav2vec 2.0 systems that train with the encoder in the loop were run with one seed. LoRA was not evaluated under the matched protocol, so the fair encoder comparison covers frozen encoders only. With 24 actors the uncertainty about new speakers stays substantial, and RAVDESS emotions are acted by professionals and are clearer than everyday conversation.
